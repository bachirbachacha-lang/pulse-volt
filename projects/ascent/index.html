T−8.9 s · Ignition

Throw mass down, go up

A rocket works by Newton’s third law. It throws hot gas out of its engines at enormous speed, and the gas pushes back. Because it carries its own oxygen, it keeps working where there is no air at all.

The five first-stage engines start in sequence and burn kerosene with liquid oxygen. Together they swallow about 13 tonnes of propellant every second. Hold-down arms keep the vehicle on the pad until full thrust is confirmed.

T+0 · Liftoff

Seven and a half million pounds of thrust

The arms release and the rocket rises slowly at first. It weighs nearly three thousand tonnes, and for the first few seconds most of its thrust is spent just holding that weight up. Clearing the tower takes about ten seconds.

T+1:22 · Max Q

The hardest push of the flight

About 13 km up, speed is climbing fast but the air is still thick. Aerodynamic pressure on the hull peaks here, at a point engineers call Max Q. The structure is designed around this moment.

From here the rocket leans over in a gravity turn, trading straight-up climb for the sideways speed it will need to stay in orbit.

T+11:49 · Orbit

Falling around the Earth

The engines cut off. The spacecraft is still falling, but it is moving sideways so fast that the ground curves away beneath it before it can land. That is all an orbit is.

The rocket equation is why this is hard: every extra kilometre per second needs exponentially more propellant. That is why the vehicle on the pad was mostly fuel.
